# Case study: letting the gate, not a human, ship the model

## Problem

Demand forecasting projects usually end at a notebook score. The hard part is the decision that
follows: is a candidate actually better than the model in production, is the improvement
concentrated in the average while the tail gets worse, and would anyone notice when the world
shifts? ModelGate implements that decision as a statistical gate with a signed audit trail.

## Data

The panel is generated by `data/generator.py` from a documented DGP: 12 SKUs, 900 days, weekly and
annual seasonality, local trend, promotions, fixed holidays, two regime-shift SKUs, and
overdispersed count noise. The generator is seeded, so the dataset hash is stable:
`2b55406a…` (see `reports/backtest.json`).

Why synthetic: the repo must prove the mechanics of leakage detection, significance testing and
drift monitoring without shipping third-party retail data. The DGP is adversarial enough to matter
(promotions and regressions), and the baselines are reported honestly.

## Method

* Features: 28 lags, rolling mean/std over 7/14/28 days, day-of-week, scheduled promo and holiday
  flags. All computed from rows strictly before the target date.
* Model: a global LightGBM next-day forecaster with a mean booster and p10/p50/p90 quantile
  boosters, used recursively for the 28-day horizon.
* Evaluation: six expanding-window folds of 28 days. WAPE, MASE against seasonal-naive, signed bias
  per horizon, pinball loss at p10/p50/p90.
* Decision: Diebold-Mariano on paired absolute errors, a seeded bootstrap interval on ΔWAPE, a
  worst-decile tail check, a per-horizon bias bound and a latency budget.

## Results versus baseline

From `reports/backtest.json` (6 folds, h=28):

| Metric | Seasonal-naive | Naive | Model |
|---|---|---|---|
| WAPE | 0.2871 | 0.3674 | **0.1565** |
| MASE | 1.20 | 1.53 | **0.66** |

From `reports/latency.json`: p95 64.8 ms against a 150 ms budget.

The model roughly halves seasonal-naive WAPE, so the improvement is not a rounding artefact. The
gate does not accept that claim on its own: the live gate record for the real backtest also has to
pass the tail, bias and latency checks.

## What the gate rejected and why

`make demo` writes two signed records:

* **Rejected** - a challenger with a better mean WAPE (0.0432 vs 0.0516) but a worst-decile WAPE
  7.29% worse than the champion. The tail condition fails and the record says so. This is the
  behaviour the repo exists to demonstrate: a demo where everything promotes is a broken demo.
* **Promoted** - a challenger that improves every condition, including the tail and per-horizon
  bias, with a DM p-value at machine precision and a bootstrap interval entirely below zero.

`make promote DEMO=1` reproduces the rejection. Both records are content-addressed and verified by
`make report` before any number reaches the README.

## What the monitor does

`make monitor-demo` scales the last 28 days of demand by 1.40. PSI flags all five monitored
features, residual KS flags the shifted residuals, and rolling WAPE rises above expectation. The
alert produces a dry-run retrain PR containing the drift evidence and the signed gate record.
Stationary draws are tested at a false-positive rate below 5% over 200 samples.

## Limits

* The data is synthetic; real POS data has stockouts, price elasticity and calendar quirks that the
  DGP does not model.
* The forecast horizon is 28 days and the model is recursive; long-horizon compounding is the main
  source of the residual per-horizon bias.
* Latency is measured single-host with an in-process client; it is a regression guard, not a
  capacity plan.
* The promotion demo uses controlled error distributions to exercise both gate outcomes; the real
  backtest numbers are reported separately and are not tuned.

## Next step

Add a second model family (e.g. a per-family hierarchical model) behind the same feature contract,
then let the gate decide between families on the same rolling-origin folds. The infrastructure does
not need to change: only the candidate and its artifact hash.
